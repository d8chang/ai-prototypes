Reset: A 15-Second Check-in for Draining Workdays

I researched, built and ran a 4-week pilot with 22 people in 6 countries. People reliably tried what Reset suggested, but most didn't come back on their own. That finding shaped v2.

The Problem

Burned-out people know they should take a break, but in the moment they lack the time or an idea of what to do, so they scroll instead and end up feeling more burned out.

Survey of 11 People in Tech

  • Biggest barrier: time (7 of 11), then not knowing what to do (3).
  • 9 of 11 scroll to unwind; only 4 of them feel better after.
  • 8 had never tried a wellness app. 10 of 11 would try a 15-second check-in.
  • Exercise and a hobby helped most (9 each); 5 named pressure around AI.

Desk Research

  • Mental health apps keep about 3% of users at 30 days.
  • Data entry is a top reason people quit.
  • I checked where HIPAA, the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule and FDA general wellness guidance apply, so Reset stays a wellness tool with no medical claims.

Design principles: under 15 seconds, no typing, no streaks, and one concrete idea instead of a menu.

What I Built

A no-login web app on Google Apps Script with a Google Sheet as the database: free to run and easy to analyze.

  1. Sign-up: first name, email, how often, and a time for the check-in email.
  2. Three taps: mood, time available, budget.
  3. One idea from 53 activities, gentler on low-mood days, with a timer or steps built in.
  4. Follow-up: "Did you do it, and how do you feel now?" gives a before-and-after mood.
  5. Weekly recap of what helped, plus one "how manageable was your week" question.
  6. Privacy by design: minimal data, and journal and vent text never stored.

The Pilot

Oct 14 to Nov 10. I invited 14 friends, then recruited strangers through product Slack communities and Reddit. 22 people signed up from the US, Chile, India, Pakistan, Egypt and Uganda.

Lesson: acceptance and completion were already above 85%, so those metrics had no room to move. Next time I'd test where people are unsure (low-energy days) or measure ideas skipped before picking.

What Testers Said

20 pieces of feedback from comments, a feedback form, annotated screenshots, an email reply and two interviews.

What Worked

  • "Love this idea! Especially the focus on small, manageable breaks without pressure." (Women in Product community)
  • The check-in felt like "a quick pulse check, not an extra task," and the calm look and colors stood out. (Interview)
  • No streaks or guilt made it a real breather, and never seeing journal notes built trust right away. (Written review)
  • Like "Hey buddy, how was your workday?" from a friendly neighbor. (Interview)
  • A friend drank a glass of water on a rough day, as suggested, and it helped. (Friend)

Where It Fell Short

8 of 20

Reminders Don't Reach People

Email gets ignored after a stressful day or lands in spam. Yet check-ins often came within minutes of an email, so the reminder works when it's seen.

8 of 20

Unclear What You Sign Up For

The sign-up didn't say what the emails contain, and "budget" read like paying for the app.

5 of 20

No Reason to Come Back

Testers asked for a weekly payoff in plain counts, or a fresh tip instead of scores.

3 of 20

Ideas Don't Fit the Moment

"I am at my desk so not feasible to spend time on hobby." Reaching out to a friend doesn't suit drained days.

"Self help apps require the user to be intentional." A tester who went quiet, and wished for a quick text like "Take a deep breath!"

v2: Built Around Coming Back

  • Reach: reminders at a moment you pick ("after my last meeting") instead of email. In the phone app, each notification carries one small action you can do right there, with Did it and Check in buttons.
  • Fit: "Where are you?" (home, office, outside); "Up for people, or just you?" on low days; no budget question.
  • Payoff: a weekly page in plain counts ("Walks helped you 3 of 4 times").
  • Fast: loads in about half a second, down from about 5 seconds in v1, which a tester flagged as a reason people click away.
  • Calm and trust: a private journal, optional rain, brook or lo-fi café sound, and all data kept on the phone.

What v2 tests: does a reminder at a chosen moment bring people back in week 2?

Caveats

  • Small sample: 22 people and 73 check-ins; one person moves retention by 8 points. Read these as direction, not proof.
  • A few people carry the data: the three most active testers made 36 of the 73 check-ins.
  • Friends and goodwill: the week-1 group is mostly friends; retention with strangers is likely lower.
  • Nudges and spam: I sent three personal reminders, and some early emails landed in spam.
  • Self-reported mood with no control group, counted only when people answered the follow-up.

What I Learned

  1. Measure return, not starts. Mixing in late joiners hid the drop. Following one group told the real story.
  2. Pick metrics with room to move. Both A/B tests used metrics already near the top.
  3. The channel is part of the product. Spam and ignored emails hurt retention more than anything inside the app.
  4. Small data shows direction; interviews explain it.
  5. Say only what's true. v2 only says an idea "has helped you" after two of your own ratings.
